# C3 passes, for the first time: salience detects held-out emergences it has never seen

2026-09-04. Scores `docs/plans/prereg-salience-c3-v3.md`, committed at
`1127fb4` before this scorer existed. `scripts/test_salience_c3_v3.R`.

**Criterion 1 passes. All refusal clauses hold. This is the first time C3 —
the salience gate's real positive test — has run and passed.**

The original document's C3 (fed2010–2019, chained Trends windows) was flagged
as untested and never run. Its amendment (nsw2023/fed2025/sa2026) turned out
to have an emergence population of 5, not the assumed 9, four of five
concentrated in One Nation in one state, tripping its own refusal clauses
before scoring. This is the third attempt, and the first to reach a runnable
result.

17 usable held-out emergences across 8 election clusters (fed2010, fed2013,

## Why this one is trustworthy where the amendment was not

Population size and diversity, both fixed before scoring: 8 election clusters
against the amendment's 2, and party composition IND 11/ONP 3/GRN 2/OTH_RIGHT
1 among the 17 usable rows — One Nation is 18%, not 80%. The result surviving
a complete drop of sa2026 (getting *stronger*, 3.377 against 3.226) is the
clearest evidence that this is not a repeat of the amendment's problem in a
different costume.

Every emergence in the population is a name most readers of Australian
politics will recognise: Andrew Wilkie's first win in Denison (2010) and his
later win in Clark (2019, same person, different seat), Rob Oakeshott, Clive
Palmer, Cathy McGowan, Rebekha Sharkie, Helen Haines, Zali Steggall, and
South Australia's 2026 One Nation breakthrough. None was picked to make the
result look good — this is the full population, not a sample from it.

## What "passing" means here, precisely

The model moves every one of these 17 candidates toward their actual result,
by a modest but consistent amount (typically 3–4 points on a base that was
usually at or near zero). This is deliberately smaller than a raw-jump model
would produce — the percentile feature that makes cross-era comparability
structural also discards magnitude information, so a candidate 100× louder
than their seat's field and one merely loudest by a whisker both read as
`xp ≈ 1.0`. The trade this whole document exists to make is real: less
individual predictive punch, in exchange for a result that can honestly be
pooled across four regions and sixteen years rather than assumed comparable.

## What this does and does not settle

**Settles**: salience, measured as a within-election search-interest
percentile, predicts genuine first-time seat winners better than uniform
swing alone, out of sample, across every region and era this repo has data
for, not just the federal cases the model was fitted on.

**Does not settle**: whether to ship the percentile-based gate in place of
the current raw-jump version. That is a separate decision with a real cost to
weigh — the raw-jump model, while its cross-era comparability is unproven,
produces much larger and more useful individual corrections (Dai Le moved
from 1.85 to 15.07 under raw jump against roughly a 4-point move under
percentile for a comparable case here). A hybrid — percentile for anything
compared across eras or regions, raw jump within a single already-fetched
election where the anchor is fixed and known — is one option not evaluated
in this document.

## What this cannot see

Same limits `prereg-salience-c3-v3.md` names in advance: 17 observations
still carry the whole result; sa2026 is still the largest single contributor
even after widening (5 of 17); the simple uniform-swing baseline is not what
the published harnesses actually use; and qld2020/2024 plus four WA cycles
were checked directly and confirmed to contribute nothing real (zero
non-major Assembly winners in WA across those years, five returning
incumbents in QLD) rather than being silently skipped.

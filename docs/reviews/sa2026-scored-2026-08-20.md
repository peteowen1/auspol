# The two-party framework broke in South Australia, and the gap question is still open

Run 2026-08-20. Two results. The one I went looking for is weak; the one I did
not is load-bearing for Victoria 2026.

## The result I did not go looking for

`2026sa.txt` marks **44 of 47** South Australian districts as `classic` — a
Labor-versus-Liberal contest. The election delivered **21**.

| final two | districts |
|---|---:|
| ALP v ONP | **24** |
| ALP v LIB | 10 |
| LIB v ALP | 3 |
| LIB v ONP | 2 |
| ONP v ALP | 2 |
| ONP v LIB | 2 |
| IND v ONP | 2 |
| IND v LIB | 1 |
| IND v ALP | 1 |

**One Nation made the final two in 32 of 47 districts.** The Liberal Party made
it in 18, down from 47 of 47 in 2022.

So a pre-election classification of which seats are two-party contests — made by
the same source this repo takes its margins from — **was wrong for 26 of 47
seats**, and wrong in one direction: it assumed the Liberals would be there.

Victoria 2026 is forecast with One Nation near 21% of the statewide primary
vote. South Australia was 22.9%.

## Why this is not just a labelling problem

Where the Liberals miss the final two, a two-party swing is not merely
unrecorded, it is **undefined**. There is no Labor-versus-Liberal contest to
measure a swing in. That is why only 21 districts can be scored at all, and it
is why the scorable ones are not a random sample:

| | districts | mean prior Labor margin |
|---|---:|---:|
| scorable (Liberal made the final two) | 21 | **−5.73** |
| not scorable | 26 | **+13.23** |

**The seats that remained two-party contests are the Liberal-leaning ones.**
Selection on precisely the variable that makes the election unusual.

And the surviving contests behaved unlike any election in the corpus:

| | baseline dispersion (MAE) |
|---|---:|
| qld2020 | 1.676 |
| vic2018 | 3.153 |
| vic2022 | 3.351 |
| nsw2019 | 3.399 |
| nsw2023 | 4.508 |
| **sa2026 (21 districts)** | **9.146** |

Twice the dispersion of the most volatile election otherwise on record. Mean
swing +2.88 with a standard deviation of **10.47**. This is a realignment, not a
swing.

## The gap question, which this was supposed to settle

Refusal **G1** of [../plans/prereg-gap-decay.md](../plans/prereg-gap-decay.md)
named the confound: every short-gap election in the corpus follows federal 2022,
so "short gap helps" and "federal 2022 was unusual" predict identically. sa2026
is short-gap (10 months) on **federal 2025**, so it separates them.

| measure | uniform | with `fed_swing` | gain |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| transposed | 9.1459 | 8.6522 | **+0.494** |
| published | 9.1459 | 8.7043 | **+0.442** |

Positive on both, and the largest **absolute** gain of any election in the
corpus. That points away from federal 2022 being the explanation.

**But the metric decides the answer, and no rule for sa2026 was
pre-registered.** As a *share* of baseline error:

| election | gap | gain | as % of baseline |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| vic2022 | 6 | +0.337 | **10.1%** |
| nsw2023 | 10 | +0.391 | **8.7%** |
| **sa2026** | **10** | **+0.494** | **5.4%** |

On the relative measure sa2026 sits **below both** short-gap elections. The
absolute and relative readings disagree, this election's baseline is twice
anyone else's, and the pre-registration covered sa2018 and sa2022 only.

**So G1 is weakened, not broken.** Anything stronger would be choosing the
metric after seeing which one gives the answer — which is the specific failure
`CLAUDE.md` records twice.

## What should actually happen next

Not more work on the gap. It is a question about a coefficient worth roughly a
tenth of a point of MAE.

**The `classic` assumption is worth more.** The published Victorian forecast
takes which seats are two-party contests from a pre-election classification, and
the nearest comparable election broke that classification in 55% of its
districts. Whether the Victorian model degrades gracefully when a seat stops
being a two-party contest is not known here, and is checkable.

That connects to what was already recorded three days ago in
[onp-allocation-sa-2026-08-17.md](onp-allocation-sa-2026-08-17.md) and to
`simulate_seats()` only simulating seats where both incumbent and challenger are
majors — the reason One Nation cannot win a Victorian seat in this model by
construction. **This adds the measurement that was missing from that argument:
not that One Nation won four seats, but that it made the final two in 32 of 47
while the Liberals collapsed from 47 to 18.**

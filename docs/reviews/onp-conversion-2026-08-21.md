# One Nation converts every primary lead it gets. The argument is about how many it gets.

Measured 2026-08-21. **Nothing changed.** `scripts/conversion_rate.R`.

The point of this is not to match YouGov. It is to find out which model is
better described by the one completed election where One Nation contested at
the level Victoria is forecasting.

## The number nobody had measured

South Australia, March 2026, One Nation on 22.87% statewide:

| leader on first preferences | seats led | seats won | conversion |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ALP | 35 | 34 | 97.1% |
| LNP | 6 | 5 | 83.3% |
| **ONP** | **4** | **4** | **100.0%** |
| IND | 2 | 2 | 100.0% |

**45 of 47 primary leads converted.** One Nation converted every one of its
four, on an average lead of 33.8% of the primary vote.

**This refutes the expectation it was run to test.** The prediction going in was
that preferences would punish One Nation — that it would lead widely and lose
most of them, because two majors preference against it. That is not what
happened. It led in very few places, and won everywhere it led.

## So the disagreement is concentration, not conversion

If leads convert at ~100%, then a seat forecast for One Nation is almost
entirely a forecast of **how many seats it leads on primaries**. On that
statistic, at a near-identical statewide vote:

| | share of seats where One Nation leads |
|---|---:|
| **South Australia 2026, actual** (22.9%) | **8.5%** (4 of 47) |
| ours (22.9%) | 14.6% |
| ours (24.0%) | 19.5% |
| **YouGov, Victoria** (24%) | **34.1%** (~30 of 88) |

**YouGov is four times the only real observation.** We are about twice it, and
sit between.

## YouGov's numbers disagree with South Australia twice over

They report One Nation leading on primaries in roughly 30 of 88 seats and
winning **17** — a **57%** conversion, with the Coalition taking four of its
leads and Labor nine, "as One Nation struggles to attract preferences".

South Australia says leads convert at **100%**. So on that election's evidence,
either their 30 leads is too high, or their 57% conversion is too low. **Both
cannot be right, and their combination is not what the one comparable election
produced.**

## Where our own number is honestly uncomfortable

We are also more concentrated than South Australia — 14.6% of seats against its
8.5% at the same statewide vote. That is worth stating rather than resting on
being closer than YouGov.

**It has a plausible and checkable cause.** Leading is not only about One
Nation's own vote, it is about who else is in the seat. South Australian Labor
polled **37.5%** in 2026; our Victorian forecast has Labor near **25%** and the
Coalition near **28%**. One Nation on 30% leads far more often against weak
majors than against a party on 37.5%. So a higher lead share in Victoria is
expected, and the question is whether twice is the right amount.

**Not established here.** It is the same allocation SHAPE question that sits at
the top of the queue, now with a sharper form: the shape governs how many seats
One Nation leads, that is what decides its seat count, and it is fitted on one
election.

## What can be said on the page

Our lower One Nation seat count is **not timidity**. It follows from a
concentration closer to the only completed election at this level than the
alternative on offer, and from a conversion rate that election measured at
100%, which we and YouGov both have to answer to.

That is a claim with evidence behind it. It was not available yesterday, and it
is the honest version of "we do not have to match YouGov".
